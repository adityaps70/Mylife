What to count as an asset

Use today’s value, not what you paid.

  • Savings accounts and cash: the balance today, plus cash at home and wallet balances.
  • Fixed and recurring deposits: the amount deposited so far, plus interest already credited.
  • Mutual funds and shares: the current value in your fund or demat statement, not the amount invested.
  • EPF, PPF and NPS: the latest balance in your passbook or statement. Easy to forget because you never see this money, and often the largest financial asset a salaried person has.
  • Gold and jewellery: the weight at today’s gold price. Leave out making charges; you don’t get them back on resale.
  • Property: a realistic selling price today. The home you live in has its own box, so it can be shown separately.
  • Vehicles and other assets: the resale value of a car or bike, and money others owe you that you really expect back.

Leave out things that have no resale value to speak of (furniture, phones, clothes) and term insurance, which pays nothing while you are alive.

What to count as a liability

  • Loans: the outstanding principal today, not the original loan amount and not EMI × months left (that includes future interest). Your lender’s app or loan statement shows it; the EMI calculator can estimate it.
  • Credit cards and pay-later: everything you owe right now, including spends that are not billed yet and any card EMIs.
  • Other borrowings: gold loans, loans against an FD or a policy, and money borrowed from family or friends.

The second is what you could spend or invest without selling the roof over your head; SEBI’s investor net worth calculator takes the same view and lists real estate as “not the house you live in” (as of 24 November 2026). The third is your cushion if your income stops: EPF, PPF and NPS are locked in, and gold and property take time to sell.

How to raise your net worth

  • Clear the most expensive debt first. Every rupee of principal repaid raises net worth by a rupee, and on a credit card it also stops interest of around 3–3.75% a month. See the credit card interest calculator and the debt payoff planner.
  • Save before you spend. Decide the amount on payday; the 50/30/20 budget calculator gives a starting split.
  • Know where the month goes. Net worth only rises if you spend less than you earn, and that takes an expense tracker or the free monthly budget sheet.
  • Check it monthly. One reading means little. Twelve readings show the direction.

Questions

How do I calculate my net worth?

Add up everything you own at today’s value (bank balances, deposits, mutual funds and shares, EPF, PPF and NPS, gold, property, vehicles) and subtract everything you owe (home, car, personal and education loans, credit card dues and other borrowings). Net worth = total assets − total liabilities.

Should I count the house I live in?

Both ways are used, which is why this calculator shows both. Your home is an asset, so the total includes it. But you can’t spend it without moving out, so the second figure leaves out the home and its loan. SEBI’s own net worth calculator lists real estate as “not the house you live in”.

Do EPF, PPF and NPS count towards net worth?

Yes. They are your money, so they are part of your net worth. They are locked in for years, though, so this calculator does not count them as money you can reach in a few days.

Can net worth be negative?

Yes. If your loans and card dues add up to more than what you own, net worth is negative. It is common just after taking an education loan or early in a home loan. The useful question is whether it rises month after month.

How often should I check my net worth?

Once a month is enough for most people, on the same day each month, so that salary and EMI dates don’t distort the comparison.

Is my data saved or shared?

The numbers are kept only in your own browser so they are still there when you come back. Nothing is sent to Self Streak and no sign-up is needed. Use “Clear the form” to remove them.
