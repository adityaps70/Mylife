Budgeting

The 50/30/20 budget rule in India, with rupee examples

The 50/30/20 rule splits your take-home pay into three buckets: needs, wants and savings. It is simple enough to follow without a spreadsheet, and it works on Indian salaries with a few adjustments.

By the Self Streak team · Published 2026-11-13 · Updated 2026-11-13 · 4 min read

Short answer: The 50/30/20 rule puts 50% of take-home pay on needs (rent, groceries, bills, EMIs), 30% on wants (eating out, shopping, trips) and 20% on savings and extra debt repayment. On a ₹75,000 salary that is ₹37,500 for needs, ₹22,500 for wants and ₹15,000 to save.

What is the 50/30/20 rule?

The 50/30/20 rule is a budgeting guideline popularised by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book All Your Worth. It divides your monthly take-home pay (salary after tax, PF and other deductions) into three parts:

  • 50% needs: things you must pay to live and work. Rent or home loan EMI, groceries, electricity, phone and internet, school fees, insurance premiums, commuting, and minimum payments on loans.
  • 30% wants: things you choose. Eating out, Swiggy and Zomato orders, OTT subscriptions, shopping, gadgets, holidays.
  • 20% savings: emergency fund, SIPs, PPF, extra loan prepayments and any goal you are saving for.

Saving ₹15,000 a month adds up to ₹1.8 lakh a year before any returns. At ₹8,000 a month, six months of savings builds a ₹48,000 emergency cushion.

If eating out creeps up to ₹11,000, the plan still works as long as another want gives way. The point is not to hit every line exactly but to keep the three totals in balance.

How to tell a need from a want

A simple test: if you stopped paying it, would your health, home, job or credit suffer? If yes, it is a need. A basic phone plan is a need; the latest phone on EMI is mostly a want. Groceries are a need; daily food delivery is a want.

Minimum loan and credit card payments count as needs because missing them hurts your credit. Anything you pay above the minimum to clear debt faster counts as savings.

Build an emergency fund first

An emergency fund is money set aside for job loss, a medical bill or an urgent repair. A common target is three to six months of essential expenses, which for the example above is ₹90,000 to ₹1,80,000. Until it exists, put most of the 20% here, in a savings account or liquid fund you can reach within a day. Without it, the first surprise expense goes on a credit card and the budget falls apart.

Adjusting the rule for Indian cities

In Mumbai, Bengaluru, Delhi NCR or Pune, rent alone can take 30–40% of a starting salary, which makes 50% for needs hard to hit. Two common adjustments:

  • 60/20/20: allow 60% for needs, cut wants to 20%, and keep savings at 20%.
  • Savings first: move your savings amount to a separate account or SIP on salary day, then split the rest between needs and wants.

Keep the 20% savings target if you can. If you cannot, start at 10% and raise it by 1–2% with every increment.

Where do EMIs fit?

EMIs on a home, car or personal loan are needs, because missing them hurts. A widely used rule of thumb is to keep total EMIs under about 30–40% of take-home pay. If EMIs alone take more than half your salary, the 50/30/20 split will not fit until you reduce debt. A debt payoff plan helps you choose which loan to clear first.

How to start in 15 minutes

  1. Find your take-home pay from your last salary slip.
  2. Multiply by 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2 to get your three limits.
  3. List fixed needs (rent, EMIs, bills). If they exceed 50%, use 60/20/20 for now.
  4. Set up an auto-transfer or SIP for the savings amount on salary day.
  5. Track spending for one month and compare it with your limits.

Tracking is the step most people skip. An expense tracker that logs spends by voice, photo or bank statement makes it take seconds a day.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Budgeting on gross salary. Use what actually reaches your account.
  • Forgetting yearly costs. Insurance premiums, school fees, festivals and annual subscriptions. Divide them by 12 and set that amount aside every month.
  • Counting credit card spends when you pay the bill. Count a spend in the month you make it, or you will think you have more money than you do.
  • Treating every want as a need. Be honest about food delivery, upgrades and subscriptions.
  • Giving up after one bad month. Look at a three-month average before changing the plan.

Common questions

Is the 50/30/20 rule based on gross or net salary?

On take-home (net) pay: what reaches your bank account after tax, PF and other deductions. Your PF contribution is extra saving on top of the 20%.

Does the 20% include investments like SIPs?

Yes. Savings covers your emergency fund, SIPs, PPF, NPS contributions you make yourself and extra loan prepayments.

What if I cannot save 20%?

Start with any fixed amount on salary day and increase it with each raise. Consistency matters more than the exact percentage.
