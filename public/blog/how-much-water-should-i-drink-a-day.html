How much water should you drink a day?

There is no single number that fits everyone, but there are good reference values and a simple way to work out your own target, with extra for hot Indian summers and workouts.

By Aditya Pratap Singh · Published 2026-11-13 · Updated 2026-11-13 · 3 min read

Short answer: European Food Safety Authority guidance puts adequate total water intake at about 2.0 litres a day for women and 2.5 litres for men, from drinks and food together, with roughly 70–80% coming from drinks. A simple personal guide is about 30–35 ml per kg of body weight, plus more in heat or after exercise.

How much water do adults need?

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) set adequate intakes of total water at 2.0 litres a day for women and 2.5 litres a day for men in temperate conditions with moderate activity. “Total water” includes water from food. Since food usually provides around 20–30% of it, that works out to roughly 1.5–1.6 litres from drinks for women and 2.0 litres for men.

These are averages for healthy adults. Heat, humidity, physical activity, pregnancy, breastfeeding and illness all raise the need.

Water intake by body weight

A simple per-kg guide many people use is about 30–35 ml of fluid per kg of body weight a day. At 33 ml per kg:

Extra for Indian summers and exercise

Sweat losses rise sharply in hot, humid weather and during exercise. Practical adjustments:

  • Hot or humid day: add about 0.5 litre (two glasses).
  • Exercise: add roughly 0.4–0.8 litre for each hour of moderate workout, sipping during and after.
  • Heavy sweating: plain water alone may not be enough; nimbu paani with a pinch of salt, coconut water or ORS replaces electrolytes.

Water from food

Food provides a meaningful share of daily water, and many Indian foods are water-rich: dal, sambar, rasam and curries, curd and buttermilk, cucumber, tomato, watermelon, oranges and khichdi. A meal with dal, curd and salad adds noticeably more water than a dry snack. That is part of why the drinks target is lower than the total water figure.

Common myths

  • “Wait until you are thirsty.” Thirst works well for most healthy adults at rest, but in heat, during exercise and in older age it can lag behind.
  • “Tea and coffee dehydrate you.” In normal amounts they still add to fluid intake overall.
  • “More water is always better.” Beyond your needs it simply passes through, and very large amounts at once can be harmful.
  • “Clear urine is the goal.” Pale yellow is enough; completely clear urine all day can mean you are drinking more than you need.

Signs you need more water

  • Dark yellow urine (pale straw colour is the usual goal).
  • Thirst, dry mouth, headache or feeling tired in the afternoon.
  • Urinating rarely during the day.

Too much water is also possible. Drinking very large amounts in a short time can dilute the body’s sodium, which is dangerous. Spread intake across the day rather than drinking litres at once.

When to follow your doctor instead

People with kidney disease, heart failure, liver disease or some hormonal conditions may be told to limit fluids. Some medicines also change how much you should drink. If a doctor has given you a fluid limit, follow it over any general guide, including this one.

How to drink enough without thinking about it

  1. Drink a glass when you wake up, before tea or coffee.
  2. Have a glass with every meal.
  3. Keep a 1-litre bottle on your desk and finish it twice a day.
  4. Use reminders that adapt to what you have already drunk, not a fixed timer.

At work, while travelling and while fasting

Long meetings, train journeys and busy shifts are when intake drops. Carry a bottle and set a simple rule, such as finishing half of it by lunch. On days of religious fasting that allow water, drink steadily through the allowed hours rather than all at once. On fasts without water, avoid heavy exercise and heat, and break the fast with water and something salty, such as nimbu paani or buttermilk.

Common questions

Do tea, coffee and milk count?

Yes. All drinks count towards fluid intake. Moderate tea and coffee still hydrate overall, though water is the best everyday choice.

Is 8 glasses a day right?

Eight 250 ml glasses is 2 litres, which is close to the reference values for many adults but too little for a large or active person in summer.
