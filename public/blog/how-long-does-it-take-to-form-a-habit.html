How long does it take to form a habit?

You have probably heard that it takes 21 days to form a habit. The best-known study on the question found something different, and the details are more encouraging than the myth.

By Aditya Pratap Singh · Published 2026-11-13 · Updated 2026-11-13 · 4 min read

Short answer: In a 2010 study at University College London, new daily habits took a median of 66 days to become automatic, ranging from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the habit. Missing a single day did not meaningfully set people back.

What the research found

In the study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London, 96 volunteers each chose a new healthy behaviour to do once a day in the same situation, such as eating a piece of fruit with lunch or going for a 15-minute walk before dinner. For 12 weeks they reported every day whether they did it and how automatic it felt.

For the people whose data showed a clear pattern, automaticity rose quickly at first and then levelled off. The time to reach that plateau had a median of 66 days, and individual results ranged from 18 to 254 days. Simpler behaviours, like drinking a glass of water, tended to become automatic faster than harder ones, like doing sit-ups.

Where does “21 days” come from?

The 21-day figure is usually traced to a 1960s self-help book by the plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz, who observed that patients took a minimum of about 21 days to adjust to changes. Over time “a minimum of about 21 days” became “21 days”. It was never a measured result for building habits.

Does missing a day break a habit?

Not much. In the UCL study, missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit-forming process. What matters is coming back the next day. The real risk of a missed day is the story you tell yourself afterwards (“I’ve broken it, so why bother?”), not the missed day itself.

That is why a good habit tracker should show your streak and your overall consistency, so one miss does not erase weeks of effort.

How to make a habit stick

  1. Pick one specific cue. “After I brush my teeth at night, I read for 10 minutes” beats “read more”. The study’s habits were tied to a daily situation for this reason.
  2. Start smaller than you think. Five push-ups or one glass of water every morning builds the routine; you can grow it later.
  3. Same time, same place. Consistent context is what makes a behaviour automatic.
  4. Track it visibly. Ticking a habit off and seeing a streak gives an immediate reward that the long-term benefit cannot.
  5. Plan for misses. Decide now: “If I miss a day, I do it the next day, no matter what.”
  6. Give it two to three months. Expect around 66 days for a daily habit, longer for hard ones. If it still feels like effort after three weeks, that is normal.

Do streaks help or hurt?

Streaks help because they turn an invisible long-term benefit into something you can see today. A 30-day streak feels worth protecting. They can backfire when a single miss feels like failure and you quit. Two ways to keep the benefit without the trap:

  • Track consistency as well as streak: “26 of the last 30 days” still shows a habit forming.
  • Set goals in days per year, such as “gym on 200 days this year”. A missed day lowers the pace slightly instead of resetting everything to zero.

Habit stacking with Indian routines

Habit stacking means attaching a new habit to one you already do every day. Daily routines give natural anchors:

  • After morning chai: 5 minutes of stretching.
  • After puja or prayer: write down the day’s top three tasks.
  • While the pressure cooker whistles: log today’s spends.
  • After dinner: a 10-minute walk, a common evening habit in many families.
  • After locking the door at night: put the phone on charge outside the bedroom.

Why reminders at the right time help

A reminder works best when it arrives at the moment the habit should happen, not at a random time. A gym reminder at 6:30 pm, sunscreen at 7:30 am and reading at 9:45 pm each match their cue. Self Streak’s habit tracker reminds you at each habit’s own time, skips the reminder if you have already done it, and keeps your streak and goals counting.

Review once a week

A five-minute weekly check keeps habits from quietly fading. Look at which habits you did most days, which you missed, and why. If a habit keeps failing at the same point, change the cue or make it smaller instead of relying on willpower. Drop a habit that no longer matters to you; fewer habits done well beat a long list you ignore.

Common questions

Is it 21, 66 or 90 days?

None of these is a rule. The median in the best-known study was 66 days, with a huge range. Plan for two to three months for a daily habit.

Are weekly habits harder to form?

Usually, because there are fewer repetitions to build the link between cue and action. Tie them to a fixed day and time.
