Credit card statement date vs due date: what each one means

Two dates decide what you pay on a credit card and when. Get them right and a card is an interest-free loan for up to about 50 days. Get them wrong and you pay 3–4% interest a month.

By Aditya Pratap Singh · Published 2026-11-13 · Updated 2026-11-13 · 5 min read

Short answer: The statement date is the day your card closes the month’s bill; everything you spent since the last statement is on it. The due date is the last day to pay that bill, usually 15–20 days later. Pay the full amount due by the due date and you pay no interest.

What is the statement date?

The statement date (also called the billing date) is the day your card issuer closes a billing cycle and creates your bill. Every purchase from the day after the previous statement date up to this date appears on the new statement. Anything you spend after the statement date goes on next month’s bill.

Example: your statement date is the 5th of every month. A ₹4,000 purchase on 3 March is on the 5 March statement. A ₹4,000 purchase on 6 March is on the 5 April statement.

What is the payment due date?

The due date is the last day to pay the bill created on the statement date. Most Indian cards give 15 to 20 days between the two. If your statement date is the 5th, your due date might be the 23rd or 25th.

The statement shows two amounts: the total amount due (everything on this bill) and the minimum amount due (usually around 5% of the total, plus any EMIs and fees). Only paying the total amount due keeps you interest-free.

How the interest-free period works

The interest-free period is the time between a purchase and the due date of the bill it lands on. It depends on when in the cycle you spend:

6 March (day after statement)5 April statement25 Aprilabout 50 days
20 March5 April statement25 Aprilabout 36 days
4 April (day before statement)5 April statement25 Aprilabout 21 days

So a big planned purchase made just after your statement date gets the longest time to pay. The exact number of days depends on your card’s cycle.

The interest-free period disappears if you carry a balance. RBI requires card issuers to explain in their terms that the interest-free credit period is suspended if any balance from the previous bill is still outstanding. In practice, once you pay less than the full amount, new purchases start charging interest from the day you make them.

Why paying only the minimum is so expensive

Paying the minimum due keeps your account in good standing and avoids a late fee, but interest is charged on the rest, usually 3–3.75% a month (about 36–45% a year).

A worked example: a ₹60,000 balance at 3.5% a month, paying only a 5% minimum (at least ₹500) and spending nothing more on the card, takes about 141 months (nearly 12 years) to clear and costs about ₹1,11,917 in interest, almost twice the original balance. RBI requires every billing statement to warn that paying only the minimum results in repayment stretching over months or years with compounded interest.

RBI rules that protect you

The RBI’s Master Direction on credit and debit cards (2022, updated March 2024) sets several rules worth knowing:

  • 3-day grace for late fees and credit reports. Card issuers can levy late payment charges, or report your account as past due to credit bureaus, only when the account stays past due for more than three days after the due date.
  • Late fees only on what is overdue. Late payment charges are levied only on the amount outstanding after the due date, not on the total amount due.
  • You can change your billing cycle. Card issuers must let you modify the billing cycle at least once, choosing the date that suits you, through channels like the helpline, app or internet banking.
  • Interest only on what you owe. Interest is levied only on the outstanding amount, adjusted for payments, refunds and reversed transactions.

The 3-day grace is a safety net, not a plan. Interest on the unpaid balance can still apply from the due date, so pay on time.

How to choose the best statement date

If your salary arrives on the 1st, a due date around the 5th to 10th means the bill is paid while money is in your account. Work back 15–20 days to find the statement date to ask for. If you hold two cards, staggering their due dates spreads payments across the month.

When does a payment count as made?

A payment counts when your card issuer receives it, not when you send it. Payments through the card issuer’s own app, UPI or net banking to the same bank usually reflect the same day. Payments from another bank, by cheque or through third-party apps can take one to three working days, and weekends and bank holidays can add more.

The safe habit is to pay at least two to three working days before the due date, and check that the payment shows on your card account. Keep the payment reference until it does. If the due date falls on a Sunday or holiday, do not assume it moves to the next working day; check your statement or ask your card issuer.

Refunds work the other way. RBI rules say a credit from a refund, failed or reversed transaction before the due date should be adjusted against the amount due, so a large refund can reduce what you need to pay. Check the updated amount in your card app before you pay.

How to never miss a due date

  1. Set up an auto-debit for at least the minimum due, as a safety net.
  2. Pay the full amount manually a few days before the due date.
  3. Track what you have already spent on the next bill, so it is not a surprise.
  4. Get a reminder that shows the amount, not just the date.

Common questions

Is the statement date the same as the due date?

No. The statement date closes the bill; the due date, usually 15–20 days later, is the deadline to pay it.

What happens if I pay one day late?

Under RBI rules, late fees and credit bureau reporting apply only if the account is past due for more than three days. Interest on the unpaid balance can still be charged, and the interest-free period on new purchases is lost until you clear the balance.

Does paying the minimum due affect my credit score?

Paying at least the minimum on time avoids a late payment mark. But a high balance relative to your limit can still lower your score, and the interest adds up quickly.
