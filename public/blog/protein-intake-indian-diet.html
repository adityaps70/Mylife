How much protein do you need on an Indian diet?

Protein is the nutrient many Indian diets fall short on, especially vegetarian ones built around rice and roti. Here is how much you need and how to get it from everyday Indian food.

By Aditya Pratap Singh · Published 2026-11-13 · Updated 2026-11-13 · 3 min read

Short answer: ICMR-NIN’s 2020 nutrient requirements for Indians set the recommended protein intake for healthy adults at about 0.83 g per kg of body weight a day, so about 58 g for a 70 kg adult. People who train regularly often aim higher, around 1.2–1.6 g per kg.

How much protein do Indian adults need?

The ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition’s 2020 report on nutrient requirements for Indians recommends about 0.83 g of protein per kg of body weight a day for healthy adults, assuming good-quality protein. The average requirement behind it is about 0.66 g per kg; the recommended figure adds a margin so it covers nearly everyone.

The higher columns are common targets for people who exercise or lift weights; they are not part of the ICMR-NIN recommendation. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need more, and people with kidney disease should follow their doctor’s limit.

Getting enough protein as a vegetarian

A typical vegetarian day of tea, poha, dal-rice, roti-sabzi and a snack often lands around 35–45 g of protein, below the target for most adults. The fix is to add one protein-rich item to each meal rather than overhaul the diet:

  • Swap one katori of rice for an extra katori of dal, rajma or chole.
  • Add curd or a glass of milk to lunch or dinner.
  • Add paneer, tofu or soya chunks to one sabzi a day.
  • Snack on roasted chana, peanuts or sprouts instead of biscuits.
  • Combine cereals and pulses (dal-chawal, rajma-rice, idli-sambar): together they give a better mix of amino acids.

Protein quality: why dal-chawal works

Protein quality depends on how well a food supplies the essential amino acids your body cannot make. Cereals like rice and wheat are low in lysine; pulses are low in methionine. Eaten together, as in dal-chawal, rajma-rice, khichdi or idli-sambar, they cover each other’s gaps. Milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fish and chicken provide complete protein on their own. You do not need to combine foods at every meal; across the day is enough.

If you eat eggs, fish or chicken

Non-vegetarian foods make the target easier: two eggs give about 12 g, and 100 g of cooked chicken breast or fish gives about 20–30 g. Choose grilled, tandoori or curry preparations over deep-fried ones, and keep pulses and curd in the diet for fibre and variety.

Spread protein across the day

Getting 15–30 g at each main meal is easier to digest and more useful than one big protein-heavy dinner. Breakfast is where most Indian diets are lightest, so start there.

How to check what you actually eat

Most people overestimate their protein. Tracking for even one week shows where the gaps are. Self Streak’s calorie and protein tracker estimates protein from a photo or a description like “2 rotis, dal and paneer sabzi”, and shows your progress against your daily target.

Common mistakes

  • Counting raw dal weight as cooked. 100 g of raw dal has about three to four times the protein of 100 g of cooked dal.
  • Relying on one big meal. A light breakfast and lunch rarely get made up at dinner.
  • Assuming “healthy” snacks are high-protein. Most biscuits, namkeen and fruit juices add very little.
  • Ignoring older family members. Appetite often falls with age; milk, curd, eggs and soft dals help keep intake up.

Common questions

Do I need protein powder?

Not usually. Most people can meet their target from food. Powder is a convenience if you train hard and struggle to eat enough.

Is too much protein harmful?

For healthy adults, intakes up to about 1.6–2 g per kg are commonly used by athletes. People with kidney disease should not raise protein without medical advice.
